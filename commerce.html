Numbers in commerce: prices, phone numbers and numeric domains

How businesses use digits to sell — what the research supports and what is folklore.

By the 017777 editorial team · Last reviewed 2026-10-22 · How we research

Price endings: 9, 7 and the left digit

The most robust pricing effect is the left-digit effect: buyers read the first digit and partly ignore the rest, so 19.99 feels closer to 19 than to 20. That is why 9 and 99 endings dominate retail. Seven-endings (27, 47, 97) are common in online info-products, but the case for them rests mostly on marketing habit; one long-time marketer calls it herd behaviour rather than tested fact (Blog Marketing Academy).

Round prices (20, 100) signal quality and simplicity and tend to suit premium or emotional purchases; precise prices (19.97) signal a calculated, discounted offer. The choice is a positioning decision, not a trick.

Lucky numbers change purchase intent — for some buyers

Research from Beijing found lucky price endings lifted purchase intention when buyers were in a negative mood, superstitious, felt a lack of control or had just faced an important event (Chen et al., 2022). In Singapore, superstitious endings persisted in newspaper ads — especially for expensive items and Eastern brands — and Western brands that localised their price endings were rated more positively (Gelbrich et al.).

Memorable phone numbers

A number customers can recall is free advertising. Vanity numbers spell a word on the keypad (1-800-FLOWERS style), while “golden” numbers use repeats such as 7777 or 8888. In Korea, the early mobile prefixes carried status, and when the government unified them under 010 thousands of subscribers resisted (JoongAng Daily). Numeric domain names as assets

Short numeric .com names are scarce by construction: there are only 10,000 four-digit .coms and 100,000 five-digit ones. Chinese buyers drove a well-documented boom in short and numeric names; TechCrunch reported that China had become the largest buyer of domains, with three-letter .coms that once sold for $10,000–$15,000 reaching $50,000 or more (TechCrunch). Buyers value numeric names because they cross language barriers, work on any keyboard and can carry cultural luck.

Numbers in brand names

Digits in brand names (7-Eleven, 3M, Forever 21) are short, global and easy to trademark in combination — but a bare number is hard to own. If you plan a numeric brand, pair the digits with a distinctive word or design mark and check existing registrations first.
